When people think about a career change, they often picture a blank page. Years of experience seem to vanish, and they imagine competing with someone fresh out of school. That picture is rarely accurate. The more useful idea is that of transferable skills: abilities you built in one setting that still work in another.

The difficulty is that most of us cannot name our own skills. We say "I just do my job." This post offers a practical exercise for naming your transferable skills, so that when you look at a new role you can say what you already bring.

## Why skills are hard to see from the inside

Skills you use every day feel ordinary. A customer service representative who calms an angry caller does not think "conflict resolution". An accounting clerk who spots a mistyped figure does not think "attention to detail". They think "that was Tuesday".

Job titles hide skills as well. Titles describe where you sat in an organisation, not what you could do. The same skill may appear under completely different titles in other industries.

That is why a deliberate exercise helps. You are translating the work you do into the language that other employers use.

## Step 1: Collect your examples

Set aside an hour and a stack of index cards or sticky notes. On each one, write a specific thing you did at work or outside it. Include volunteer work, family responsibilities and projects you took on without being asked.

Good cards are concrete:

- "Reconciled the monthly vendor statements and found a duplicate payment."
- "Trained two new hires on the booking system."
- "Rewrote the template we send to customers after a delay."
- "Kept a shared calendar for a team of eight, with no double bookings."

Aim for 30 or more. Do not judge them yet. If it took effort or you were pleased with it afterwards, write it down.

## Step 2: Ask what each card required

Pick up each card and ask: what did I have to be good at to do this? Write the answer in a few words on the back. For example:

- Finding a duplicate payment: checking records against each other, noticing patterns, careful follow-up.
- Training new hires: explaining clearly, patience, organising information in order.
- Rewriting a template: writing for a specific reader, tone, simplifying.
- Managing a calendar: coordinating several people, planning ahead, handling conflicts.

Try to be specific. "Communication" is too broad to be useful. "Explaining a technical process to someone new" is something you can show.

## Step 3: Sort the cards into groups

Spread the cards out and start grouping them. You will see clusters form. Common ones include:

- working with numbers and records accurately
- explaining things to people
- organising people, time or information
- spotting problems and following them through
- using software and learning new tools quickly
- handling difficult conversations

Give each group a plain-language name. If one group has ten cards and another has two, that is information too. The large groups are your strongest skills.

## Step 4: Separate skills from tools

A tool is something you used, such as a particular accounting package or phone system. A skill is what you did with it. Tools change from employer to employer, while skills usually travel.

For each group, write one sentence that does not mention any specific software. "I check financial records for errors and trace them to their source" works anywhere. "I was good at one specific system" works for one employer.

That said, tools still matter when a job posting asks for them. Keep a separate short list of the software you know. It simply belongs in a different column from your skills.

## Step 5: Compare against real job descriptions

Now take two or three descriptions of roles you are curious about. These could be postings you found, or the occupation pages on O*NET OnLine and the Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Outlook Handbook, both of which describe typical tasks and skills for hundreds of occupations.

Highlight every phrase you could back up with one of your cards. Then mark the phrases you could not. You now have two lists:

- **Skills you already have.** These go at the top of your resume and into your conversations.
- **Gaps.** These are things to learn, and they are usually fewer than you feared.

If the gap list is long, that role may be a longer move. If it is short, you may have found a realistic neighbour of your current job.

## Step 6: Practise saying it out loud

Write three sentences you could say in an interview or networking chat, each starting from a skill and ending with an example:

"I'm good at spotting problems in records. At my last job I found a duplicate payment during a monthly reconciliation and set up a check so it did not happen again."

Say them aloud a few times. It feels awkward at first, and it improves quickly. Leave out numbers unless they are real and you can explain where they came from.

## What this does not do

Naming your skills does not guarantee an interview or a new job. Employers decide on many things, and some roles will ask for qualifications or experience you do not have yet. What the exercise does give you is clarity about what you bring, a shorter list of things to learn, and language for describing both. That is a better place to start than a blank page.
