If you have typed "will AI replace my job" into a search bar at midnight, you are in good company. The trouble is that the question is too big to answer. A job title is a bundle of many different tasks, and AI affects those tasks very unevenly.

A more useful question is smaller: which parts of my week could software do, which parts need me, and what should I do about that? You can answer it with a pen, a notebook and about an hour. Here is a simple way to do it.

## Why job titles are the wrong unit

Two people with the same title can spend their days very differently. One bookkeeper spends most of the week keying in invoices. Another spends it explaining numbers to a small-business owner. Headlines about "jobs at risk" flatten that difference.

Tasks are the better unit. A task is a specific piece of work: "reconcile bank transactions", "answer a customer's billing question", "draft a weekly status email". Some tasks are repetitive and rule-based, which makes them easier to automate. Others depend on judgment, relationships or context, which makes them harder to hand over.

Looking at tasks also gives you something to act on. You cannot do much about a title. You can do plenty about a task.

## Step 1: List what you actually do

Open a notebook and write down every task you did in the last two weeks. Check your calendar, your sent email and your to-do list so you do not rely on memory alone. Aim for 15 to 30 items, and keep each one to a short verb phrase.

If you get stuck, O*NET OnLine, the U.S. Department of Labor's occupational database, publishes task lists for hundreds of occupations. For bookkeeping, accounting and auditing clerks, for example, it lists tasks such as "operate computers programmed with accounting software to record, store, and analyze information", "check figures, postings, and documents for correct entry, mathematical accuracy, and proper codes" and "reconcile or note and report discrepancies found in records". Use a list like that as a prompt for things you forgot, not as a replacement for your own.

## Step 2: Sort each task into one of three groups

Go down your list and mark each task:

- **Mostly routine.** The steps are the same each time, the inputs are structured, and a mistake is easy to spot. Data entry, formatting reports and matching records often fall here.
- **Shared.** Software can do a first pass, but a person needs to review, correct or explain. Drafting a summary, flagging unusual items or answering common questions often falls here.
- **Mostly human.** The task depends on trust, negotiation, context that is not written down, or accountability for a decision. Explaining a result to a worried colleague or deciding what to do about an exception often falls here.

Be honest but not harsh. If you are unsure, put the task in "shared". Most knowledge work lives there.

## Step 3: Look for patterns, not a verdict

Now count. How many tasks are routine, shared and human? How much of your week does each group take? You are not producing a score or a prediction. You are producing a picture.

Three patterns are common:

1. **Most of your time is routine.** Your day-to-day work is probably the most exposed to change. That is useful to know early, while you have time to move toward the shared and human tasks around it.
2. **Your time is mostly shared.** Your job is likely to change shape rather than disappear. Learning to review and direct automated work well becomes part of the job.
3. **You already do a lot of human work.** Those tasks are a base to build on, and they are often what employers look for in adjacent roles.

None of these patterns tells you what will happen to you. They tell you where your attention is best spent.

## Step 4: Pick one move for each group

For each group, choose one small action this month.

- **Routine tasks:** learn how the tools in your field already handle them. Try one yourself so you understand what it does well and where it makes mistakes. Someone who can check automated output is more useful than someone who ignores it.
- **Shared tasks:** practise the review step. Where do errors creep in? What would you need to look for? Write it down as a short checklist.
- **Human tasks:** collect examples. A decision you explained well, a problem you spotted early, a customer you calmed down. These become the evidence for your next conversation with a manager or recruiter.

## Step 5: Look at neighbouring roles

Once you can see which of your tasks are durable, ask which other roles use more of them. Many roles share skills with yours. An accounts payable clerk who spends time chasing exceptions has experience that overlaps with internal controls work. A customer service representative who is good at untangling problems has skills that carry into customer success.

O*NET lists related occupations for each role, and the Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Outlook Handbook describes what those roles do day to day and how they are expected to change. Treat both as starting points for your own research, and read the details before you decide anything.

## Repeat it in six months

Tools change quickly. The list you make today will look different next spring, so keep it. Re-sort it every six months and note what moved. Over time you will see a trend in your own work instead of reacting to each new headline.
