Accounting looks backwards: it records what happened and makes sure the books are right. Financial planning and analysis, usually shortened to FP&A, looks forwards: it asks why results differ from the plan and what is likely to happen next. If you are a junior accountant curious about the FP&A analyst career path, the good news is that you already hold much of what the work asks for.

This guide explains what FP&A analysts do, which of your skills carry over, and what is worth learning. It is general career information, so check job postings in your own area before you plan around any of it.

## What an FP&A analyst does

"FP&A analyst" is a job title used by employers rather than a formal occupation in government data, and its exact duties vary by company. The closest category in the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Outlook Handbook is financial analysts, who research and evaluate financial data, forecast future trends and prepare reports with recommendations.

In practice, inside a company, that often looks like:

- building and updating budgets and forecasts with the finance team
- comparing actual results to the budget and explaining the differences
- preparing monthly reports and summaries for managers and executives
- answering questions such as "why did operating income miss plan?"
- modelling "what if" scenarios in spreadsheets

Notice how much of this is communication. The numbers matter, but so does the ability to turn them into a short explanation a busy manager can act on.

## What the outlook looks like

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reports a 2025 median pay of $103,570 per year for financial analysts, and projects employment in that group to grow 7 percent from 2025 to 2035, which it describes as much faster than average for all occupations. These are national figures for a broad category. They are not a promise about any one job, employer or city, and pay varies widely with location, industry and experience. The BLS lists a bachelor's degree as the typical entry-level education for the category, though employers differ in what they ask for.

## Skills that carry over from accounting

You may be surprised by how much overlaps.

- **Reading financial statements.** You already know how the income statement, balance sheet and cash flow statement connect. Many people entering FP&A from other fields have to learn this first.
- **Accuracy and reconciliation.** Habits like checking totals, tracing a number back to its source and noticing when something looks off are valuable when you are the person explaining the numbers.
- **Knowledge of the chart of accounts.** Understanding how transactions are categorised helps you find the real cause of a variance.
- **Month-end familiarity.** You know when the data is reliable and when it is still moving.
- **Spreadsheet skills.** If you already use lookups, pivot tables and formulas daily, you have a head start.

The BLS also notes that financial analysts are expected to be needed to assess growing volumes of data, which is one reason comfort with data tools is worth building.

## Skills worth building

Here is where most accountants need to stretch.

**Variance analysis.** This means comparing budget to actual, finding the largest differences and working out what drove them. A useful habit is to separate price effects from volume effects, and one-off items from trends.

**Forecasting.** Accounting asks "what happened". Forecasting asks "what is likely next, and how sure are we?" Start with simple methods, such as extending a trend and adjusting for known events, before you reach for anything complicated.

**Business context.** Learn what drives your company's revenue and costs. Talk to people in sales, operations and product. A variance is much easier to explain when you know what happened on the ground.

**Writing and presenting.** Practise a five-bullet summary: what happened, why, what it means, what you expect next, and what decision is needed. Short and clear beats long and thorough.

**Modelling.** Learn to build a simple driver-based model in a spreadsheet, where a few inputs such as headcount, price and volume produce the outputs. Keep it tidy and documented so someone else can follow it.

## A realistic way to start from where you are

You do not have to wait for a job title change to begin.

1. **Volunteer for variance work.** Ask whoever prepares your monthly budget-to-actual report if you can draft the commentary. Even one paragraph a month builds the skill and creates examples you can describe later.
2. **Learn the forecast process at your company.** Ask who builds it, what the inputs are and how often it is revised. Sit in on a review if you can.
3. **Practise on public data.** Pick a public company's quarterly results and write a half-page explanation of why revenue or margin moved. Keep it in a folder.
4. **Tidy your resume around analysis.** Describe what you did with the numbers, not only what you processed. Use real results only. If you do not have a figure, leave it out.
5. **Talk to someone in the role.** A short conversation with an FP&A analyst, ideally at your own company, is worth more than hours of reading.

## What the first role might look like

Titles vary. Some companies hire "financial analyst" or "FP&A analyst" at an entry level and expect you to learn the forecast process on the job. Others look for a year or two of related experience, which is where an accounting background helps. Reading several postings side by side will show you what your market asks for, and which gaps come up most often. Make a list and tackle the most common ones first.
