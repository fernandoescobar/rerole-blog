If you are wondering how much AI could change your own work, a quick AI exposure check is a sensible first step. It is also easy to misread. A result like that is a starting point for thinking. It is not a prediction about your future.

This post walks through what ReRole's free AI exposure snapshot does, what you will see at the end, and, just as important, what it cannot tell you.

## What "AI exposure" means

Exposure is about tasks, not people. A task is exposed when software could plausibly do some or all of it, such as sorting records, drafting routine text or summarising a report. A role with many exposed tasks is likely to change. That does not mean the role will disappear, and it says nothing about whether you personally will keep or lose a job.

That is why a good AI job risk check talks about parts of your work. Your role is a mix of tasks. Some are more exposed, some less, and many sit in between and will change in how they are done rather than whether they are done.

## How the snapshot works

The free snapshot takes about two minutes. You do not need an account or a credit card.

1. **Pick your current role.** ReRole models ten starting roles: Junior Accountant; Bookkeeping or Accounting Clerk; Administrative Assistant; Customer Service Representative; Telemarketer or Inside Sales Representative; Accounts Payable or Receivable Clerk; Claims Processor; Junior Paralegal or Legal Support; Basic Marketing or Copywriting Role; and Entry-Level Data or Reporting Analyst.
2. **Answer a few questions about how you spend your time.** These are about the shape of your week, not personal details.
3. **See your results.**

If your role is not on the list, the snapshot will not cover it yet. ReRole is adding roles based on demand.

## What you see at the end

The results page has three parts.

**Exposed tasks.** The tasks in your role that are most likely to be affected by AI. Seeing them named can be a relief, because worry about "my job" often turns into something more specific and manageable.

**Human strengths.** The tasks that remain firmly human, such as judgement, relationships and accountability for decisions. These are the skills worth keeping sharp and talking about.

**Where your skills could take you next.** A set of example career matches, which are adjacent roles that use skills you already have. In the full product these come from a curated career map, ranked by skill overlap, demand, AI resilience and time to readiness, and each timeline is labelled as an estimate.

## How the AI is and isn't used

It is fair to ask what is doing the work behind a tool like this. In ReRole, careers come from a curated career map, and scores are calculated by fixed, transparent rules. The AI does not pick your careers or decide your scores. It is used to explain results, to coach, and to give feedback on open-ended answers against a rubric.

That matters because you can see why a result looks the way it does, rather than taking a black-box verdict on trust.

## What the snapshot does not tell you

This is the more important list.

- **It does not predict what will happen to you.** It looks at tasks in a role. It cannot know your employer, your team, your industry's pace of change or your own plans.
- **It does not say a role is "safe".** Nothing is. Some tasks are less exposed or more resilient, and that is the most that can honestly be said.
- **It is not a credential.** The snapshot is not a certification, and it is not something employers recognise or accept as a qualification.
- **It does not replace your own judgement.** You know your workplace. If a result surprises you, treat that as a question to explore, not a conclusion.
- **It does not cover every role.** Only the ten starting roles above are modelled today.

## How to use the result well

A few ideas for the day after you take it.

1. **Write down the exposed tasks you spend the most time on.** Those are where change is most likely to reach you first.
2. **Pick one human strength and gather two examples.** A moment when you used judgement or handled a difficult situation is evidence you can use in a review or an interview.
3. **Look at one suggested match with curiosity.** Read a description of the role from a neutral source such as the Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Outlook Handbook or O*NET OnLine. Ask whether the daily work appeals to you.
4. **Share nothing you are not ready to share.** The snapshot is for you. Use it however helps.

Take it again in six months. Tools and workplaces change, and the second result is a useful comparison with the first.
